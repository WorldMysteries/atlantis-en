Chapter I

The Legend of the Vanished Island

According to Plato, Atlantis was a vast island lying “in front of the Pillars of Heracles” — beyond the Strait of Gibraltar, in the Atlantic Ocean. The island was larger than Libya and Asia combined, and from it one could cross to other islands, and from there to the opposite continent.

The island was ruled by the descendants of the god Poseidon and a mortal woman named Cleito. Poseidon fell in love with her and, to protect her, encircled the hill where she lived with concentric rings of water and land — two of earth and three of water. Thus the capital with its legendary layout came into being.

The Atlanteans were noble and virtuous as long as their divine nature endured. But when it “dissolved” in their human nature, they grew arrogant, greedy, and warlike. Their empire attacked Athens and Egypt — and was defeated by the Athenians.

Then Zeus, “the god of gods, who watches over the laws,” decided to punish the Atlanteans. In a single day and a night of misfortune, terrible earthquakes and floods swallowed both the Athenian army and the entire island of Atlantis.

Chapter II

Sources: Plato and the Chain of Tradition

The only primary source on Atlantis is two of Plato’s dialogues. No author before him ever mentions the island. Plato himself claimed the story had been passed down through a chain of transmission from Egyptian priests.

Timaeus

Here the story of Atlantis is told briefly. The Egyptian priest says to Solon: “O Solon, Solon! You Hellenes are forever children, and there is no old man among the Hellenes” — for the Greeks do not remember their own glorious past.

The priest relates how, 9,000 years earlier, ancient Athens repelled an invasion from a power coming “from the Atlantic sea,” and how the island then perished, and the sea in that place became unnavigable because of mud.

“

Critias

An unfinished dialogue containing the most detailed description of the island: its geography, capital, temples, army, laws, and royal power. The text breaks off at the point where Zeus gathers the gods to announce the punishment of the Atlanteans.

It is Critias that became the source of all the details that have stirred imaginations for centuries: orichalcum, rings of canals, the temple of Poseidon, bulls, ten kings.

“This island was larger than Libya and Asia combined… But later, when the time came for unprecedented earthquakes and floods, in a single terrible day and night all your military might was swallowed by the gaping earth; and likewise Atlantis vanished, sinking into the deep.”

— Plato, Timaeus, 24e–25d

Chapter III

The Capital of the Atlanteans

Concentric Rings

The central island was 5 stadia in diameter (about 925 m). It was surrounded by three water rings, 1, 2, and 3 stadia wide, and two land rings equal in width to the adjacent water rings. The total diameter of the city, including the rings, was 27 stadia — nearly 5 km.

The Atlanteans dug a canal 3 plethra wide (≈ 90 m), 100 feet deep, and 50 stadia long from the sea to the outer ring, turning it into a harbor. Tunnels were cut through the land rings, with bridges over which triremes could pass.

The walls of the outer ring were coated with copper, those of the middle ring with tin, and the wall of the acropolis shone with orichalcum, “flashing with fiery light.”

Temple of Poseidon and Cleito

On the acropolis stood a temple a stade long (185 m) and three plethra wide. On the outside it was clad in silver, and its acroteria in gold. Inside, the ceiling was of ivory, decorated with gold, silver, and orichalcum; the walls, columns, and floor were entirely of orichalcum.

At its center towered a golden statue of Poseidon in a chariot, driving six winged horses — so enormous that its head touched the ceiling. Around it stood a hundred Nereids on dolphins.

Outside, the temple was surrounded by golden statues of all ten kings and their wives. Nearby sprang two sources — one warm, one cold — with baths for the kings, the citizens, the women, and even the horses.

Harbors and Sea Power

The harbors were “full of ships and merchants arriving from all quarters,” and day and night there was “a roar, a din, and a babel of many voices.” The outer water ring was entirely given over to shipyards: docks for triremes were hewn into the rock.

The military fleet of Atlantis numbered 1,200 ships. For supply, each of the 60,000 plots of the plain provided a fixed military contingent: a sixth of a war chariot, two horses with riders, heavily armed soldiers, archers, slingers, and four sailors.

The total strength of the army, by calculations drawn from the text, exceeded a million men — a figure plainly meant to underscore the grandeur of the empire.

The Great Plain

The city lay on the southern shore of a vast plain measuring 3,000 × 2,000 stadia (≈ 555 × 370 km), surrounded by mountains “that surpassed all present ones in majesty and beauty.” The mountains shielded it from the northern winds.

The plain was encircled by a giant canal, a plethron deep, a stade wide, and 10,000 stadia long. From it, at every 100 stadia, straight canals 100 feet wide branched off, connected by cross channels — forming a network for irrigation and the floating of timber.

The land yielded two harvests a year: in winter from rains, in summer from the canals. The island was rich in timber, elephants, fruits, and “fragrant substances.”

Orichalcum — The Metal of the Atlanteans

Orichalcum (Greek ὀρείχαλκος, “mountain copper”) was a legendary metal that “was mined from the bowels of the earth in various places on the island” and “in value was second only to gold.” By Plato’s time it was known “only by name.”

Modern scholars believe it was a natural alloy of copper and zinc — a kind of brass. In 2015, off the coast of Sicily, in the wreck of a ship from the 6th century BC, 39 ingots of an alloy of copper, zinc, lead, nickel, and iron were found — the press called them “orichalcum.”

On a column of orichalcum at the center of the temple were inscribed the laws of Poseidon, before which the ten kings swore their oaths and rendered judgement.

Chapter IV

Society, Power, and Rites

The Ten Kings

Poseidon and Cleito bore five pairs of twins. The eldest, Atlas, received the central portion and supreme power — the island and the ocean are named after him. The other nine ruled their own domains. According to Plato, their names were: Atlas and Gadeirus (Eumelus), Ampheres and Evaemon, Mneseus and Autochthon, Elasippus and Mestor, Azaes and Diaprepes.

The Laws of Poseidon

The kings were forbidden to take up arms against one another and were obliged to come to each other’s aid if anyone attempted to overthrow the royal house. Decisions on war were made jointly, and a king could not put a relative of another king to death without the consent of the majority. The laws were inscribed on a column of orichalcum.

The Rite of the Bull

Every five and six years in turn, the ten kings gathered at the temple. Without weapons, armed only with staves and nooses, they caught a bull from the sacred herd, sacrificed it over the column so that its blood ran over the inscriptions, and swore to judge according to the laws. The judgement was rendered at night, in dark blue garments, with the fire extinguished.

Life of the Island — In Detail

The island yielded hard and fusible metals, including orichalcum. Its forests supplied timber for building; the plain fed “numerous domestic animals and wild beasts,” including elephants. There grew roots, herbs, trees, flowers, and fruits “for delight and pleasure.” Plato especially notes grapes, grain, legumes, and fruits “with a hard rind.”

Stone was quarried in three colors — white, black, and red — directly from beneath the central island and the land rings, while docks were cut into the rock at the same time. Buildings were constructed either of a single tone or of variegated stone “for pleasure.” On the rings lay gardens, gymnasia, a hippodrome a stade wide, barracks of the guard, and sanctuaries of the gods.

Plato describes a three-tier guard: the greater part lived on the outer land ring, the most loyal on the inner, nearer the acropolis, and the most trusted of all directly in the citadel around the royal apartments. This detail reflects the theme of trust and political stability, important to Plato’s philosophy.

“As long as the divine nature endured in them, they regarded everything but virtue with contempt and did not prize wealth… But when the portion of the god was exhausted and the human character prevailed, they proved unable to bear their wealth and lost their sense of propriety.” For Plato this is the key to the whole story — a tale of hubris (ὕβρις) and its punishment.

Few recall that Plato also describes the “ideal” proto-Athenians: a state with a class of guardians, common property, moderation, and justice — the embodiment of his Republic. Athens single-handedly stopped the Atlanteans and liberated the enslaved peoples. The destruction of Athens in the same cataclysm is symbolic: even the perfect state is subject to cosmic cycles.

Chapter VI

Where Atlantis Has Been Sought

Over 2,400 years, more than 50 locations have been proposed. Below are the best-known hypotheses, with an assessment of their plausibility.

What Science Says

The overwhelming majority of historians and archaeologists regard Atlantis as a philosophical fiction of Plato’s. The arguments:

  • Before Plato, no source mentions Atlantis — not Herodotus, who visited Egypt, not Homer, not Hesiod.
  • Aristotle, Plato’s pupil, according to Strabo, considered the island an invention: “He who invented it, made it disappear.”
  • Geology: there is no trace of a sunken continent in the Atlantic; oceanic crust is fundamentally different from continental crust.
  • 9,000 years before Solon is the Paleolithic, when neither Athens, nor Egypt, nor ships existed.

Plato used Atlantis as a teaching example — an illustration of how an ideal state (proto-Athens) confronts a maritime empire corrupted by wealth. Many see in it an allusion to:

  • The Persian Wars and the Athenian maritime empire, destroyed by its own arrogance in the Peloponnesian War;
  • The destruction of the island of Helice in 373 BC, submerged in a single night during Plato’s lifetime;
  • The Minoan catastrophe — the eruption of Thera, memories of which could have reached him through Egypt;
  • The failed Sicilian expedition of the Athenians and the ways of Syracuse, which Plato personally visited.
